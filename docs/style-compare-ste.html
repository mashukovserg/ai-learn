Chapter 1: Why Checking Output Becomes the JobГлава 1: Почему проверка вывода становится работой

Imagine you hire a crew to renovate your kitchen. In the first days you watch every step. A few weeks later the crew handles the routine on its own, and your time goes to a different job: at the end of the day you walk in, look at what was done, and decide whether it is right. The work did not disappear. It moved from doing to checking.

The same shift is happening with language models. On 2 October 2026 Andrej Karpathy, formerly director of AI at Tesla and one of the founding members of OpenAI, wrote that as models improve they do more of the legwork on their own, and more of our work moves up into “oversight and understanding”. That is an observation, not a measurement, but it has a measured side.

You can only check what you understand, and that has been tested. In Anthropic’s experiment of January 2026, 52 mostly junior engineers wrote two features with the async Python library Trio and then answered questions about the code they had just written. The group with an AI assistant scored 50%, the group that coded by hand 67%. Inside the assisted group, the people who asked the model follow-up questions scored higher than those who only collected the code. Understanding did not arrive with the code; it came from the questions.

Karpathy’s post is about one practical part of asking: the form of the answer. The same explanation can arrive as a dense paragraph, as plain controlled text, as a diagram, as an interactive web page or as a short narrated video. He lists them in that order, each one better than the last. This room takes the four formats one chapter at a time: what each one is, how to ask for it, and what can go wrong with it.

One idea runs through all of them. The richer the format, the easier it is to take in — and the harder it is to check. A paragraph can be compared with its source line by line, while a video cannot be skimmed at all. So the useful habit is not “always ask for the most impressive format”, but to pick the format that fits the question and to check it at the simplest layer where an error can hide.

Chapter 2: Plain Text on Request — ASD-STE100Глава 2: Простой текст по запросу — ASD-STE100

Picture an aircraft mechanic in Lisbon reading a manual written by an engineer in Seattle. English is not the mechanic’s first language, the manual uses words like “commence” and “prior to”, and a single sentence packs three conditions and two actions. If the mechanic misreads the order of the steps, the result is not a typo but a part installed wrong. In the late 1970s European airlines asked the aerospace industry to solve exactly this problem.

The solution was a controlled language: a version of English with a fixed word list and fixed rules for building sentences. A working group of AECMA, the European aerospace industry association, started on 30 June 1983, and the first guide came out on 15 February 1986. Since 2005 the specification has been called ASD-STE100, Simplified Technical English. The current edition, Issue 9 of 15 January 2025, is a free download. It has two parts: 53 writing rules and a dictionary of about 900 approved words, where each word has one meaning and one part of speech — “start”, not “commence”; “use”, not “utilize”; “before”, not “prior to”.

Many of the rules are measurable, which is why the standard travels well beyond aviation. Here is the summary page of Section 6, on descriptive writing:

Three numbers on this page do most of the work: 25 words per sentence, one topic per paragraph, six sentences per paragraph. Other sections add more. An instruction has at most 20 words and one action. A noun cluster — a chain like “hydraulic reservoir pressure sensor” — has at most three words. Writers use the active voice and call one thing by one name throughout.

Karpathy’s tip is to ask a model to explain something in ASD-STE100. He notes that models know the standard well, and its limits remove what makes model prose heavy: long sentences, stacked clauses, synonyms for variety. Because the full standard is strict, he sometimes asks for “80% of the way to ASD-STE100”: the limits on sentences and paragraphs stay, but the text no longer has to sound like a maintenance manual. A working request looks like this:

Request
Explain how this function handles retries. Write 80% of the way to ASD-STE100. Keep the technical terms.

The method has limits. ASD-STE100 is a specification for English only, and there is no Russian equivalent. You can ask a model to follow the same principles in Russian, but the numbers do not carry over directly: Russian has no articles, so 25 Russian words hold more content than 25 English ones. And plain text is still text — it is easier to read, but its claims still have to be checked against the source.

Анимации Сандерсон делает в Manim — собственной Python-библиотеке с открытым кодом (лицензия MIT, на GitHub с 2015 года); с 2020 года рядом развивается форк сообщества, Manim Community. Раз сцена Manim — это код на Python, её может написать модель.

Конвейер состоит из четырёх шагов: сценарий (что говорится и что показывается), код анимации, озвучка и рендер. Для озвучки нужен сервис синтеза речи — в примере Карпати это ключ API ElevenLabs — или речевая модель, которая работает на вашем компьютере. Каждый шаг может сломаться отдельно: код не запустится, голос неправильно произнесёт термин, анимация покажет то, чего нет в сценарии. Оценка самого Карпати осторожная: он пишет, что это только начинает работать.

Видео труднее всего проверить. Его нельзя пробежать глазами или найти в нём нужное место поиском, а ошибка в озвучке проходит за секунду. Поэтому проверка переезжает туда, где ошибку поймать дешевле всего, — в сценарий. Ни одна из них не заменяет понимание — они меняют скорость, с которой вы к нему приходите. Some ideas are about change in time. For example, a gradient moves a point down a slope. A matrix stretches space. A picture shows only one moment. A paragraph describes the movement, but you must imagine it. An animation shows the movement, and a voice explains it at the same time.

Karpathy likes this format the most. He wants an explainer video that a model makes for any topic. His example request asks for a video “in the style of 3b1b”. 3b1b is 3Blue1Brown. It is the YouTube channel of Grant Sanderson, with animated explanations of mathematics.

Sanderson makes the animations with Manim, his Python library. Manim is open source with the MIT licence, and it is on GitHub since 2015. Since 2020, a community version, Manim Community, also exists. A Manim scene is Python code. Thus, a model can write it.

A video has four steps:

  • A script: what the voice says and what the screen shows.
  • The animation code.
  • The voice audio.
  • The render.

For the voice, you need a speech service or a speech model on your computer. The example from Karpathy uses an ElevenLabs API key.

Each step can fail. The code can stop with an error. The voice can say a term incorrectly. The animation can show something that is not in the script. Karpathy is careful about the result. He writes that the method only starts to work now.

A video is the most difficult format to check. You cannot read it quickly, and you cannot search in it. An error in the voice goes past in one second. Thus, the check must occur at the script. To make a video that you can trust, do these steps:

Procedure
  1. Tell the model to write the script first.
  2. Compare each statement in the script with the source.
  3. Then tell the model to make the animation and the voice.

The four formats make a ladder: text, diagram, page, video. Each step up is easier to understand and more difficult to check. Each format also has a text level: the sentences, the diagram code, the data table, or the script. To use the ladder, do these steps:

Procedure
  1. Select the lowest step that fits the question.
  2. Use a diagram for a structure, a page for a comparison, and a video for movement.
  3. Check each format at its text level.
